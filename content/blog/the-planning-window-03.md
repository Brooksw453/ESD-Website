## What I'm seeing this week

When a compliance plan stalls, the instinct is to assume someone isn't working hard enough. Almost every time I look closely, that's not it. The coordinator is working plenty hard. The structure is the problem.

Here is the shape of it. Accessibility is assigned to one person. That person can see everything that needs to happen. But the website is Marketing's. The LMS is IT's. The documents belong to individual faculty. Procurement is its own office. The coordinator owns the *responsibility* for all of it and has *authority* over none of it, and controls none of the budgets. So they send emails, build decks, and ask nicely, and the plan sits there — not because anyone refused, but because nothing about the structure makes it move.

The institutions where this actually moves are not the ones with a more heroic coordinator. They're the ones where accessibility is attached to something that already has authority — a cabinet-level sponsor who can say "this is happening," or a line written into an existing governance body's charter so it shows up on an agenda that people answer to.

If your program depends entirely on one person's persistence, it is one resignation away from zero. That is a structural risk, and you fix it with structure, not with more of that person's evenings.

## One thing you can do in five minutes

Find the one body at your institution that already has authority and a recurring meeting — IT governance, a provost's council, a risk or compliance committee. Email the chair two sentences: ask to add a standing accessibility item, with you giving a short status each meeting.

You are not asking anyone to create a new committee or hand you a budget. You are attaching accessibility to a table that already has teeth. That single move does more than a year of polite reminders, because now it lives somewhere people have to answer for it.
